Ping Pong Player

Project Overview

This project involves the HiWonder ArmPi Pro interacting with a dynamic environment, in the form of moving objects of interest, using computer vision, trajectory planning, and inverse kinematics to enable a player to engage in various gameplay.

MVP & Stretch Goals

MVP: Our original MVP was to have a fixed robot arm block a ping pong ball rolling towards it. The arm should be able to track and predict the ball’s trajectory, plan out the kinematics for where to go to contact the ball, and successfully make it there in time across varying ball speeds.

Stretch Goals: The introduction of timing-based elements serve as a way to go beyond our original MVP. This can be done in two main ways if we remain on the rolling surface: we can either “return” the ball, meaning to hit the ball in a manner such that it gains a velocity to send it backwards on the table, or “catch” the ball, meaning to open the end effector, or gripper, and close it when the ball within grasp. These two challenges are ordered based on perceived difficulty through the required timing accuracy, as a return has a greater window of interaction than a catch with the HiWonder’s grippers. Beyond this, we will hopefully move beyond the planar problem of a rolling ball and move into three dimensions by being able to make contact with a ball that is mid-air. To this extent, the previous MVP-stretch goal distinction exists, as first making contact with a mid-air ball comes before returning and or catching.

Why These Changes: For our MVP, we have a well-working prototype (though we are missing tuning and any verifiable metrics). Given this, we think the MVP is feasible to be reasonably achieved within the next week of working on the project, allowing us more time to reach our stretch goals. The stretch goals themselves seem reasonable, although certain elements may not be a direct extension of previous work, i.e. planning for 3-D geometries, while it is a simple extension mathematically, requires a newly developed testing setup and lends itself to greater perceived work.

Status & Major Components

Overall, we have verified that the pipeline can work in full on all of the required hardware. We got an overhead camera (placed flat on top of a table) to stream footage over Wi-Fi via DroidCam, process this via a Python script to detect and locate the ball, then predict the intersection between this ball and a “block line” in front of the robot, and send the end effector to that position over UDP. Vision

Unfortunately, we were having difficulty streaming frames from the RealSense cameras and capturing any frame for processing via USB connection. To counteract this, we simply used an IPhone 15 as our camera, where DroidCam allowed us to connect and stream the footage wirelessly to feed into our vision pipeline (this was deemed faster than debugging the initial stump that the RealSense gave us given a member’s prior experience using this method for an external camera, though this added about 150ms of delay, measured via pointing the phone at a timer on the laptop’s screen). To ensure stability, we simply placed the phone flat across a wooden block hanging over a table for maximal vision, with the robot on the floor.

For the actual computer vision processing for our project, we tackled two main issues: firstly, isolate and extract the ball’s position, and then using this pixel position, translate this into the robot’s frame. For the isolation and extraction, we got the ball’s center via HSV thresholding, and then a few morphological operations, such as erosion and dilation, as a form of image processing to remove noise and smooth out our image. Then, with a masked image that represents the balls as a “blob”, we find the largest based on size and average the x and y values of this blob to find the center. One issue with this testing is that the robot’s hue was deemed as within this threshold when the light shined on it at a particular angle, making certain interactions quite jittery.

For the transforms, we found the homography, using four printed AprilTag markers that were defined 60mm in size. Using OpenCV’s built-in homography finding function, we could now map any pixel to meters on the table’s (or in this testing case, floor’s) plane. We then needed to convert the table coordinates into the robot’s frame. We recorded, by hand, the distances between the top left corners of all the tags relative to one another. Defining the bottom left tag and the bottom left corner of this tag as our world origin, we measured the robot’s center, or the middle of the rotating plate, distance to the origin. This allows us to describe the points that are in the table relative to the robot, and since this is a planar problem, we use one fixed height that is guaranteed to make contact with a rolling ball without potentially driving into the floor. Trajectory Generation

We applied a theoretical physics model to our ball’s rolling path. Given that the ball rolls smoothly across the table, the only forces acting upon the ball would be gravity, the normal force, and friction. The only force here that impacts the ball is friction, and with this assumption, our ball would experience a constant deceleration proportional to friction. We independently solve this across each axis given that our ball moves in the x-y plane. We define the equation of the ball as p(t) = p0 + v·t + ½·a·t2. We fit the ball’s positions to this equation via least squares across the last 0.6s of detections, which gives us a recent update of the fitted trajectory. The velocity and acceleration are simply found via the fit, where we are consistently determining a dt value between this point and the last point, where p(dt) = our function. This fit also assumed an acceleration of 0 unless there are more than 15 points, since the acceleration was very noisy. When the ball is lost, it resets.

To then predict the interception, we define a “block line”, which is just a line that sits in front of the robot. Given we have an equation for the ball’s x and y, we can hardcode the blockline to sit at a particular x value (to be in front of the robot) and solve for the y-coordinate of the intersection between block line and ball’s trajectory. We validated this visually and through the functionality of our MVP.

Kinematics

The kinematics for our project were an extension from previous work that has been done in the Fundamentals of Robotics course at Olin, developed by both Enzo and Vanessa (previous students). This work can be observed here: https://github.com/enzolaj/funrobo-miniprojects. This work was later adapted to integrate more than just the Raspberry Pi, which operates the HiWonder robot. Taken together, all of the inverse kinematics (numerical) could simply be extracted away. To this extent, we simply set up communication between the host laptop and the listener Raspberry Pi, which would listen for a 3-D coordinate (which we get from our intersection finder, with a fixed x and z as the IK solver needs three inputs) to input into the inverse kinematics solver.

We found that the UDP communication takes around 2ms, the IK takes around 15ms, and moving the end effector has a maximal speed of around 0.8m/s, though we experimented with 0.6m/s to be on the safer end. Taken together, this means that it would take around 0.17 seconds from the command to reach the point. We recorded the arm moving between two points multiple times in order to see if any of the speeds reached a limit, where we noticed that after 0.6m/s, the jitter from moving so fast was intense. Potential Risks

Fundamentally, our current CV approach is prone to misalignment across testing environments as it is purely tuned by hand. The HSV approach is also likely to falter under the same testing environment as moving shadows and various small lighting modifications could greatly impact the detection, which would make further testing inconsistent unless we are more intentional with our actions. Currently, we also don’t have a solid plan for how we could tackle erratic jumps in our predicted intersection, as we try to remove outliers from the fit, but have no logic for what comes after the fit. This could emerge as a noisy prediction that mitigates other attempts to refine accuracy and go unnoticed. We also aren’t sure if certain things are possible with our given speeds, such as catching the ball in our later stretch goal. Also, if we still cannot get the Realsense to work, the WiFi jitter could lead to delays, as there were from time to time some occasional lagspikes.

The largest risk for the later advances in our project are establishing a consistent and robust testing environment such that certain variables, such as robot transforms, are consistent across each use. We also may need new hardware, which could lead to a bottleneck in our ability to get started (if we deem that we need a depth-camera, or our current cameras are insufficient for the mid-air attempts).

Goals for Milestone 2

One big goal for the next milestone is to retune our HSV values for better accuracy. We only now realized that a lot of the detection may have been off since the livestream itself and previous IPhone footage were not of the same exposure value, perhaps due to some artifacts between file extension conversion. We also plan to make the arm more responsive, currently, it exhibits blocking behavior that sleeps between each target destination, which means a modification to the ball is not reacted to. Flatly, we also need to port all of this work to exist within the ROS framework we established earlier. Finally, we will also begin work on adapting the newer algorithms, such as returning and catching the ball. This will require us to be more intentional with timing. In parallel, we will develop a more robust testing setup, and perhaps use two cameras or a stereo camera to make the depth extraction easier for the switch to mid-air tracking.
